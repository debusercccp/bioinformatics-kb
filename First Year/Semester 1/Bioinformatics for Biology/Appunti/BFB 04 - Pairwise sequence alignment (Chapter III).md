# Pairwise sequence alignment (Chapter III)

## 4.1 Why align sequences

The logical chain that justifies the whole operation:

- Sequence similarity ⇒ structural similarity.
- Structural similarity ⇒ functional similarity.

Alignment therefore probes evolutionary, structural and functional relationships at once, both on nucleotide sequences (DNA) and on amino acid sequences (proteins). It is the most fundamental operation in bioinformatics:

- It decides whether two proteins or genes are related functionally, structurally or evolutionarily.
- It identifies domains or motifs shared between proteins.
- It is the basis of BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990 — the most cited paper in bioinformatics).
- It scales up to phylogenetic analysis and whole-genome comparison.

## 4.2 Protein or nucleotide?

**Proteins are usually more informative**:

- 20 characters instead of 4, and many amino acids share related biophysical properties, so a mismatch can still be informative.
- Codons are degenerate: changes in the third position often do not alter the specified amino acid, so nucleotide differences accumulate without signal.
- Protein sequences offer a longer evolutionary “look-back” time. Example: searching plant DNA with human beta globin DNA (`NM_000518.5`) yields no matches, while searching plant proteins with the human beta globin protein (`NP_000509.1`) does return matches.

**Nucleotides are more appropriate when** you need to study non-coding regions (introns, intergenic regions), DNA polymorphisms, the evolution of a fast-evolving organism (e.g. SARS-CoV-2), or the quality of a genome sequencing run.

## 4.3 Nomenclature of evolutionary relationships

| Term | Definition | Example |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Homologs** | General term for similar sequences, usually derived from a common ancestor | Globin family |
| **Orthologs** | Sequences generated by a **speciation** event | Myoglobins across species; ACE2 human vs zebrafish |
| **Paralogs** | Sequences derived from a **duplication** event | The globin genes within human |
| **Analogs** | Sequences with similar features but separate evolutionary origin | — |
| **Inparalogs** | Paralogs within the same species | Human H1.1 and human H1.2 |
| **Outparalogs** | Paralogs in different species | Human H1.1 and chimpanzee H1.2 |

## 4.4 Definitions used when reading an alignment

- **Pairwise alignment**: the process of lining up two sequences to achieve maximal levels of identity, in order to assess the degree of similarity and the possibility of homology.
- **Homology**: similarity attributed to descent from a common ancestor. It is a *hypothesis about history*, not a measurement — a sequence is either homologous or not, never “70% homologous”.
- **Identity**: the extent (%) to which two sequences are identical, e.g. 99.0%.
- **Conservation**: changes at a specific position that preserve the physico-chemical properties of the original residue (e.g. aspartate replaced by glutamate). In BLAST output, conservation is what the `+` signs in the consensus line mark.
- **Similarity**: the extent to which two sequences are related, based on identity **plus** conservation.

## 4.5 General approach

1.  Choose two sequences.
2.  Select an algorithm that generates a score.
3.  Allow gaps (insertions, deletions).
4.  The score reflects the degree of similarity.
5.  Alignments can be **global** or **local**.
6.  Estimate the probability that the alignment occurred by chance.

## 4.6 Scoring: substitution matrices

The raw score of an alignment is the sum of four contributions: matches, mismatches, gap **opening** penalties, and gap **extension** penalties. Gap opening carries a large penalty and extension a smaller one, which models the fact that one long indel is biologically more plausible than many short ones.

Match/mismatch scores come from a substitution matrix, and the choice of matrix determines whether a distant relationship is detected at all.

|  | **PAM** | **BLOSUM** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Name | Accepted Point Mutations | BLOcks SUbstitution Matrix |
| Focus | Tracking evolution | Measuring distances |
| Built from | Global alignments | Local alignments |
| Number semantics | Million years of divergence — **higher = more distant** | % similarity of the sequences used — **higher = more similar** |
| More lenient (distant sequences) | PAM250 | BLOSUM45 |
| More severe (close sequences) | PAM10, PAM1 | BLOSUM62, BLOSUM80 |

Rough equivalences: PAM250 ≈ BLOSUM45, PAM1 ≈ BLOSUM80. BLOSUM62 is the default at the NCBI BLAST site.

This is directly actionable: aligning `NP_000509.1` with `NP_005359.1` returns **no hit** under BLOSUM62, and works once the matrix is switched to BLOSUM45. A negative result is therefore not evidence of absence until the parameters have been examined.

## 4.7 Global alignment: Needleman–Wunsch (1970)

Properties: uses the entire length of both sequences; good for closely related sequences; does not scan all possible alignments, yet is **guaranteed to find the optimal solution** (dynamic programming — it finds the optimal subpath that defines the best alignment, rather than exhaustively enumerating alignments or iterating recursions).

Three steps, with the worked example Sequence 1 = `FMDTPLNE`, Sequence 2 = `FKHMEDPLE`, scores +1 match, −2 mismatch, −2 gap:

1.  **Initialise a 2D matrix** — x axis: sequence 1, y axis: sequence 2. Fill the first row and column with cumulative gap penalties (0, −2, −4, −6, …). Cells corresponding to identical residues can be marked.
2.  **Score the matrix** — for every cell take the highest of three candidates: from the LEFT (add gap score), from UP (add gap score), from the DIAGONAL (add match or mismatch score). Record which neighbour produced the best score (the arrows). First cell: left −2−2 = −4, up −2−2 = −4, diagonal 0+1 = 1 → the cell takes **1**. Second cell: left 1−2 = −1, up −4−2 = −6, diagonal −2−2 = −4 → **−1**. And so on until all cells are filled. In real life the match/mismatch values come from a PAM/BLOSUM matrix rather than from a flat ±1/−2.
3.  **Traceback** — start from the bottom-right cell, whose value is the final alignment score (here **−4**), and follow the arrows back to the top-left. Translate each step: diagonal = match or mismatch, moving down = gap in sequence 1, moving right = gap in sequence 2.

Resulting optimal alignment:

    Seq 1: F - - M - D T P L N E
    Seq 2: F K H M E D - P L - E
    Score: -4

Geometric intuition: a diagonal path through the matrix is an alignment without gaps, and every deviation from the diagonal is a gap in one of the two sequences. The procedure is not complicated but long and repetitive — the ideal task for a program; implementations exist in Linux (EMBOSS), in R, and online.

## 4.8 Local alignment: Smith–Waterman (1981)

Computes the optimal **local** alignment of two protein or nucleotide sequences, and is suited to more distant or more different sequences. The single formal difference from Needleman–Wunsch is decisive: **the score can never be negative** — wherever a negative value would be written, a 0 is written instead.

Consequences for the procedure:

- The alignment’s right end is the **maximum value in the matrix**, not the bottom-right cell.
- Traceback proceeds from that maximum until it reaches a value of 0.0, which defines the alignment’s left end.
- In two long, multi-domain sequences there can in principle be many distinct local alignments.

Example output: `GCC-UCG` / `GCCAUUG`.

EMBOSS is a suite of programs for Linux implementing these algorithms, also usable through a website (`https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/emboss/`).
