# Introduction to bioinformatics

## 1.1 Course framework

Course taught by Federico M. Giorgi (Department of Pharmacy and Biotechnology), whose research background is transcriptomics and gene networks.

- CFUs: 4 + 2 (theory + lab), for a total of 28 + 24 hours.
- Exam: written quiz on `virtuale.unibo.it`, taken in the Bioinformatics lab. No pre-registration needed; score from 0 to 31 (30 cum laude), rounded at the half point (22.5 → 23, 22.49 → 22). The quiz can be repeated, but **only the last score obtained is valid**.
- Reference textbook: *Bioinformatics and Functional Genomics*, 3rd edition, Jonathan Pevsner. Not mandatory but strongly suggested; the chapter numbering of the course follows it.
- Teaching assistant: Nicola Balboni.

Course content, mapped on the book: what bioinformatics is (Ch. 1), the NCBI databases (Ch. 2), sequence alignment (Ch. 3–4), next generation sequencing (Ch. 9), online tools (Ch. 5), phylogenesis (Ch. 7), plus hands-on Linux and R.

## 1.2 What is bioinformatics?

The NIH definition is “research, development or application of computational tools and approaches for expanding the use of biological, medical, behavioral, or health data, including those to acquire, store, organize, analyze, or visualize such data”.

The operational definition used in the course is narrower and more practical:

- Doing biological research with computational tools.
- Making sense of the huge vastity of biological data.
- Problem solving.

A useful framing given in the lecture: bioinformatics *serves* biology, but the bioinformatician does not serve the biologist. The discipline is hands-on and in constant movement (new algorithms, new tools, new data-generating technologies), so the distinction between lesson and lab, theory and practice, is deliberately blurred.

## 1.3 Purpose of the course

- Learn the basics of contemporary bioinformatics.
- Know how to run the most common tools and how to handle the most common file formats.
- If you become a biologist: be able to understand bioinformaticians.
- If you become a bioinformatician: even better.

The job market is permanently undersupplied, in Italy as well as abroad, because the required profile is rare: someone who can program, can handle big data, *and* knows and loves molecular biology (i.e. can answer “what is a gene?”).
