# The fly as a model organism

### Why the "fruit fly"?

The lecture lists seven reasons:

1.  **Very small and easy to breed.**

2.  **Short life cycle.**

3.  **Mature larvae have polytene chromosomes** in the salivary glands.

4.  **Genetic tools are available** (a century's worth of them).

5.  **Transposons have been identified** (the P element).

6.  **Transgenesis is straightforward.**

7.  **Strong genetic homology with humans** (about 50% of human genes have a fly homologue, as seen in Module 1).

**Morphology and sexual dimorphism.** The adult is about **2.5 mm** long, with **red eyes** and a brownish body with dark abdominal banding. Sexual dimorphism is clear: **males** are slightly smaller than females, have **darker banding on the posterior abdomen** and carry **sex combs on the front legs**. A female can lay about **50 eggs per day**, and the animals feed on sugar and yeast paste — which is why keeping a stock costs almost nothing.

### Life cycle

At **25 °C** the whole cycle takes about **9–10 days**: fertilization and egg deposition, then embryogenesis (roughly 24 h), three larval instars (L1, L2, L3), pupation at about 5 days, and eclosion (*sfarfallamento*) of the adult at about 9 days. Females become sexually mature at about 12 h after eclosion, males at about 9–45 h.

Embryogenesis itself is the part that matters for the screens described later. **Drosophila eggs are polarised** (anterior–posterior and dorsal–ventral axes are already defined in the egg), and development proceeds through:

- **Cleavage divisions**, which are **nuclear divisions without cytokinesis**: the fertilised egg becomes a **syncytial blastoderm**, in which the nuclei migrate to the periphery while still sharing one cytoplasm;

- **cellularisation** (~2 h), when membranes finally form around the peripheral nuclei → **cellular blastoderm**; the **pole cells** at the posterior end are the germ line;

- **gastrulation**, **germband elongation**, **parasegmental furrows**, **germband retraction**, **dorsal closure**, **head involution**, until the **final larval morphology** at about 17 h.

*Further detail:* the syncytial phase is exactly what makes the fly so convenient experimentally — until cellularisation, injected molecules (RNA, protein, dsRNA) diffuse through the **whole embryo**, and transcription factors can act as true **morphogen gradients** across a shared cytoplasm.

### Polytene chromosomes

Polytene chromosomes are found in the **salivary glands of third-instar larvae** and are one of the historical reasons the fly became a model organism.

- They are formed by **endomitosis**: repeated **replication without division**, so that each chromosome is made of hundreds of identical chromatids **lying side by side**.

- All of them are joined at a **chromocenter**, made of the heterochromatic, centromeric regions.

- **Homologues are paired along their whole length** (somatic pairing).

- They show a natural, reproducible **banding pattern**, which is what allowed cytological maps to be drawn and a mutation to be assigned to a physical position on a chromosome.

- **Puffs** are decondensed regions and indicate **actively transcribed** regions.

### A century of fly genetics

Much of classical genetics was first worked out in the fly, starting with T. H. Morgan's "fly group" at Columbia (A. H. Sturtevant, C. B. Bridges, H. J. Muller).
