# What is a model organism?

### Why a course on model organisms

Most scientific knowledge about the fundamental properties of living organisms comes from the study of model organisms. The goal is to understand the **characteristics, potential and tools** available for doing research with each of the most used organisms.

### Definition

A **model organism** is a species studied extensively to understand specific biological phenomena, on the assumption that findings from it can give insight into other organisms. The knowledge gained is **transferable**, to a greater or lesser extent, to other organisms, even very different ones, **that share an evolutionary history** with the first.

J. Monod's phrase sums up the idea: *"what is true for E. coli is also true for the elephant."* It should be read as a statement about the conservation of fundamental mechanisms, not about identical details.

### Historical model organisms

The lecture lists a series of fundamental discoveries that came from particular organisms:

- ***Pisum sativum* (pea)**: laws of heredity (**Mendel**, mid-1800s).

- ***Oenothera lamarckiana***: mutation theory (**Hugo de Vries**, late 1800s).

- ***Drosophila melanogaster***: localisation of genes on chromosomes (**T. H. Morgan**, early 1900s).

- ***Neurospora crassa***: relationship between mutations and loss of function of specific enzymes (**G. Beadle and E. Tatum**, 1941), i.e. the "one gene, one enzyme" hypothesis.

- ***Paracentrotus lividus* (sea urchin)**: interaction between gametes and embryonic development (**Oskar Hertwig**, 1875).

- ***Zea mays* (maize)**: transposons (**Barbara McClintock**, Nobel Prize 1983).

- ***Escherichia coli***: bacterial conjugation and regulation of the *lac* operon.

The common thread: an organism becomes a model because it makes a general phenomenon **easy to see** (a single species with a clear phenotype, a fast life cycle, controllable crosses).

### Key characteristics of a model organism

- easy to breed and small in size;

- **rapid development and short life cycle**;

- **large number of offspring**;

- availability of **genetic manipulation techniques**;

- experimentally accessible (for example, observable embryos);

- **genome information** available;

- commercial and research interest (scientific community, resources, stock centres).

### No organism satisfies all the criteria

The criteria conflict, so **every model is a compromise**:

- **complexity vs tractability**: the more complex the organism, the more similar to humans, but the harder to manipulate;

- **speed vs relevance**: fast models (yeast, fly) are far from humans, close ones (mouse, primates) are slow and expensive;

- **numbers vs individual analysis**: many individuals give statistical power (screens), few individuals allow detailed analysis;

- **genome simplicity vs genome realism**: small, compact genomes are easy to analyse, genomes more like ours are more redundant and complex.

### What the genomes of model organisms taught us

Sequencing showed that the number of **completely sequenced genomes** has grown exponentially (charts from 2009 and up to 2025, on a logarithmic scale, with bacteria, eukaryotes and archaea; the 2011 NCBI table shows more than a thousand complete genomes). Comparing genomes gave an important result: a large fraction of human genes has **homologue** in the model organisms. Percentages of **human genes also present in other species**:

- about **30%** in *S. cerevisiae*;

- **40%** in *C. elegans*;

- **50%** in *Drosophila*;

- **70%** in *D. rerio* (zebrafish);

- **80%** in the mouse;

- **96%** in the chimpanzee;

- about **100 human genes** are shared with bacteria.

*E. coli* scores very well on **genome sequencing, homologous recombination and biochemistry** (biochemistry "excellent"); *S. cerevisiae* and *S. pombe* have efficient homologous recombination; for *C. elegans* and *Drosophila* homologous recombination is "difficult"; *Arabidopsis* has poor homologous recombination; *Danio rerio* does not readily allow it; the mouse allows it (and it underlies knockouts by gene targeting). The meaning is practical: **the genetic tools available differ from organism to organism** and must be weighed in the choice.

### The catalogue of model organisms

- **Viruses, prokaryotes, unicellular eukaryotes, fungi**;

- **invertebrates**: *Drosophila*, *C. elegans*, *Hydra*, sea urchin, squid, *Ciona*;

- **vertebrates**: mouse, rat, chicken, *Xenopus*, pufferfish, zebrafish;

- **plants**: *Arabidopsis*, rice, maize, tobacco, *Lotus*, *Medicago*.

The classic labels (one per group): ***E. coli* "the bacterium"; *S. cerevisiae* "the unicellular fungus"; *C. elegans* "the cylindrical worm"; *D. melanogaster* "the insect"; *Ciona intestinalis* "the chordate"; *Danio rerio* "the fish"; *Xenopus* "the amphibian"; *Gallus gallus* "the bird"; *Mus musculus* "the mammal"; *Arabidopsis thaliana* "the plant"**.
