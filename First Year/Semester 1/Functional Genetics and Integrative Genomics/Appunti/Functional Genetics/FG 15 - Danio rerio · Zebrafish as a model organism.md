# Zebrafish as a model organism

### Fish in biomedical research

The lecture opens with a principle already met in the general part of the course: **the biological question determines the most useful model**. Even within fish, three species are used for very different reasons:

- **Zebrafish (*Danio rerio*)**: established genetics and fluorescent reporter lines; external development and accessible embryos.

- **Danionella (*Danionella translucida*)**: **natural transparency into adulthood**, which allows **live imaging of adult neural circuits**.

- **Guppy (*Poecilia reticulata*)**: natural variation in behaviour; a model for **sexual selection and environmental adaptation**.

### Natural history

- **Teleost** fish of the **Cyprinidae** family.

- Natural environment: **slow-moving or still waters** in the floodplains of **India, Pakistan, Nepal and Bangladesh**.

- Natural diet: insects, nematodes and other zooplankton.

### Use of zebrafish as a laboratory organism: early foundations

- **1950s – George Streisinger**: an aquarium hobbyist looking for a **vertebrate model that could be used in genetic studies**; he understood the many practical advantages of zebrafish.

- **Charles Kimmel**: zebrafish neurobiology, development and the **fate map**. His motto: *“the fish is a frog… is a chicken… is a mouse”* — i.e. what we learn on one vertebrate embryo transfers to the others.

- **1993 – the “Big Screen”** of **Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard** and **Wolfgang Driever**: male sperm mutagenised with **ENU (ethylnitrosourea)** → description of about **4000 embryonic lethal mutant phenotypes** (see Forward genetics below).

- **1994 – Bernard Thisse**: **whole-mount in situ hybridisation** on zebrafish embryos, giving more than **8,400 expression patterns**, now available in the gene-expression section of **ZFIN** (zfin.org).

**Focus — Streisinger’s real contribution.** Besides choosing the fish, Streisinger’s group produced the first **clonal lines of homozygous diploid zebrafish** by gynogenesis (1981): eggs activated with UV-inactivated sperm, then made diploid by a pressure or heat shock. This showed that a vertebrate could be manipulated genetically with the ease of an invertebrate, and is why he is regarded as the founder of zebrafish genetics.

### Why zebrafish? Advantages as a model organism

Zebrafish is a model for **vertebrate development and human diseases** (Sassen & Köster, 2015):

- relatively **cost-efficient**;

- about **70% of zebrafish genes have human counterparts**;

- **external fertilisation**;

- embryos are **transparent** and **develop rapidly**;

- **genome sequenced**;

- many **fluorescent reporter lines** available.

### The zebrafish genome

- Sequencing started in **2001** at the **Sanger Institute** and was completed in **2013**.

- About **26,000 genes** over **1.4 billion bp** on **25 pairs of chromosomes** — roughly the **same number of protein-coding genes as the human genome in about half the size**, over a similar number of chromosomes.

- **71.4% of human genes** have at least one zebrafish orthologue; **80% of human disease-related genes** have at least one zebrafish orthologue.

- Zebrafish genes underwent the **teleost-specific genome duplication (TSD)**. The fates of the duplicated copies are: **non-functionalisation** (one copy is lost), **subfunctionalisation** (the ancestral functions are split between the two), **neofunctionalisation** (one copy acquires a new function) and **dosage selection** (both kept because the extra dose is useful).

**Focus — Why the duplication matters at the bench.** Because of the TSD, a single human gene often corresponds to **two zebrafish paralogues** (named *a* and *b*, e.g. *ambra1a*/*ambra1b* in the morpholino example above). Knocking out one paralogue may give no phenotype because the other compensates, so both may have to be targeted; conversely, if the two are subfunctionalised, each mutant reproduces only part of the human gene’s function. Always check the orthology (ZFIN, Ensembl) before designing a model.

### Strains, life cycle and husbandry

**Common strains:**

- **wild type**: typical striping pattern and short fins;

- ***leo*** (leopard): mutations giving a **spot pattern**;

- ***lof*** (long fin): mutations resulting in **long, flowing fins**;

- **casper**: **lacks all melanocytes and iridophores**, both during embryogenesis and in adulthood — a transparent adult, very useful for imaging (e.g. tumours, see Cancer below).

**Focus — What casper is.** casper is a double mutant, ***nacre*** (*mitfa*, no melanocytes) × ***roy orbison*** (*mpv17*, no iridophores) (White et al., 2008). It extends to adult fish the optical transparency that wild-type embryos lose as pigment develops. *leo* encodes the gap-junction protein **connexin 41.8**.

- **Lifespan**: depends on strain and rearing, usually **3–4 years**; signs of ageing appear around **2 years**.

- **Sexual maturity**: typically **10–12 weeks post-fertilisation**.

- **Sexual dimorphism**: **females** acquire a **fuller, rounder belly** with **yellow** pigmentation; **males** have a **sleeker belly** and **orange shades** on belly and fins.

- **Small size** and **easy breeding in reduced spaces**. Mating is done in breeding tanks with a **mesh** bottom (the eggs fall through, so the parents cannot eat them) and set up to **mimic shallow water**, which stimulates spawning.

**Focus — Practical numbers.** Spawning is triggered by the onset of light in the morning; a single pair can give **hundreds of eggs per clutch**, roughly once a week. Large, synchronised clutches are what make statistics, screens and microinjection practical.
