# Animal models in biomedical research

### Definitions

**What is an animal?** Eukaryotic, multicellular, mostly heterotrophic organisms, organised to a high degree of complexity and motile during at least part of their life cycle. *Note:* the slide says "autotroph"; in biology animals are normally **heterotrophs**, so check this point with the lecturer.

**What is an "animal model"?** According to the Merriam-Webster dictionary cited: *an animal sufficiently like humans in its anatomy, physiology, or response to a pathogen to be used in medical research in order to obtain results that can be extrapolated to human medicine*. The key words are **"sufficiently like"**: the similarity must concern the very characteristic of interest.

### Why we need animals for research

Animal models are used for three purposes:

1.  **Basic research:** to expand our knowledge of the structure, functioning and behaviour of living organisms and the environment.

2.  **Applied research (translational/applied):** to address diseases through prevention and the development of treatments.

3.  **Regulatory testing:** procedures carried out to satisfy legal requirements, for example ensuring that substances are produced to legal specification and evaluating the **safety and effectiveness** of pharmaceuticals and other substances.

Animals are good models to understand how living tissues **function** and the biology underlying **disease** within a whole organism (**crosstalk among cells, tissues and organs**), with the final aim of developing treatments. The interaction of cells, tissues and organs is very complex and can often only be studied in the whole animal. Only by understanding how disease or injury affects organisms can cures be developed. Models therefore serve to: advance scientific understanding through basic research on a healthy organism; understand the fundamental ways in which diseases affect living tissues; develop and test treatments; **train** future scientists and physicians.

### Welfare, regulation and the choice of a model

Good science and good animal care go hand in hand, because **sick, stressed or mistreated animals give unreliable results**: welfare is a methodological requirement, not only an ethical one.

**The Five Freedoms** (Farm Animal Welfare Council, 1979) define animal welfare as freedom:

- from **hunger and thirst**;

- from **discomfort**;

- from **pain, injury and disease**;

- from **fear and distress**;

- to **express normal behaviour**.

**The 3Rs** (Russell & Burch, 1959) are the operating principles of animal research:

- **Replacement** with alternative methods (computer models, human volunteers, epidemiology);

- **Reduction** of the number of animals used (better experimental design and data analysis, sharing of data);

- **Refinement** of procedures to minimise pain (less invasive techniques, better care and housing).

**Reproducibility** was later added as a fourth R. The **6Rs** add **Robustness** (rigorous design), **Registration** (protocol registered in advance) and **Reporting** (ARRIVE guidelines) — the three that guarantee the scientific value of the work.

**Regulation.** In the EU, **Directive 2010/63/EU** (in Italy **D.Lgs. 26/2014**) makes the 3Rs **legally binding** and requires project authorisation, a **harm–benefit assessment**, a **severity classification**, certified personnel, an **animal-welfare body** and an **ethical review**.

### Phenotyping: what you do once the genotype has been modified

A genetic modification is only as informative as the phenotyping that follows it. In the mouse the standard battery is:

- **General screen:** **SHIRPA** (coat, reflexes, tremor, gait, locomotion and so on), plus specific tests.

- **Physiology:** metabolic cages, blood pressure, echocardiography, ECG.

- **Behaviour:** open field, social interaction, nest building.

- **Motor function:** rotarod, beam, running, footprints.

- **Sensory function:** hot plate, buried food, von Frey filaments.

- **CNS function:** wire suspension, pole test, novel object recognition.

- **Tissues:** histology, TEM, RT-PCR, Western blot.

All of these tests assume **healthy animals kept in enriched cages** — which brings the argument back to welfare.

**SHIRPA** (SmithKline Beecham, Harwell, Imperial College, Royal London Hospital, phenotype assessment): a screening procedure recommended for new mouse lines or to assess the health of individual mice. It checks many parameters: coat and whisker appearance, lacrimation, tremor, pinna and corneal reflexes, righting reflex, startle response, locomotion in a quick open-field procedure, skin and tail appearance, gait abnormalities, limb clasping, vocalisation and much more.

### Which animal model to choose?

**Similarities and differences between animals and humans are considered when choosing a model**. The criteria are:

- **genetic, genomic and evolutionary features.** The diagram of divergence times from humans (million years ago): **mouse 90, chicken 310, *Xenopus* 360, zebrafish 450, invertebrates (*Drosophila*, *C. elegans*) about 990**. The more recent the divergence, the greater the conservation in general, but the lower the speed and ease of experimentation;

- **anatomical and physiological traits**;

- **diseases** that can be reproduced in the model;

- **genetic manipulation** and **phenotyping** capabilities.

General rule of the lecture: a good model must be **similar to what it is meant to emulate (the target)** in the characteristic of interest, but also **different in other respects**, so as to allow information to be obtained that cannot be obtained in the target (for example manipulating a gene).

### Transgenic animals: definition

**Transgenic animals** are mutant animals whose **genetic material has been altered by adding, modifying or removing specific DNA sequences in their cells, including the germline**. Modification of the germline makes the change heritable and is the basis for creating stable lines of genetically modified animals (specific techniques, such as gene targeting and knockout, are the subject of later lectures).
