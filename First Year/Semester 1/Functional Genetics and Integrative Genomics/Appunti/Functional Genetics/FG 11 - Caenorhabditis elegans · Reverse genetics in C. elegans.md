# Reverse genetics in C. elegans

Reverse genetics goes **from gene to phenotype**: a known gene is altered on purpose — by site-specific mutation (CRISPR/Cas9, PCR screening of insertional libraries) or by knock-down (RNAi) — and transgenes (reporters, human disease genes) are introduced by microinjection to follow its expression or to model a disease.

### Important Notes on Gene Targeting and CRISPR/Cas9 in *C. elegans*:

- **Important Note 1 (Lack of homologous recombination):** Unlike yeast or mouse ES cells, classical gene targeting via **homologous recombination is NOT efficient in *C. elegans***.

- **Important Note 2 (CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing):** Today, precise site-specific gene targeting is performed in *C. elegans* using **CRISPR/Cas9 technology**.

- **Molecular mechanism of CRISPR/Cas9 (integrated from Module 1):** A **single guide RNA (sgRNA)** directs the **Cas9** endonuclease to a specific genomic target site adjacent to a **PAM** sequence, where Cas9 introduces a **double-strand break (DSB)**. The DSB is repaired via one of two cellular pathways:

  1.  **Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ):** An error-prone repair pathway that introduces small insertions or deletions (indels), causing frameshifts that disrupt the gene to generate a targeted **knock-out**.

  2.  **Homology-Directed Repair (HDR):** Uses a co-injected exogenous **donor DNA template** to introduce a precise sequence modification, point mutation, or fluorescent tag insertion into the endogenous locus (**knock-in**).

**Crispants (**$F_{0}$ **mosaic screening):** Injecting $F_{0}$ embryos/gonads with multiple CRISPR/Cas9 guides against the same target gene generates biallelic frameshift mutations across many somatic cells (genetic mosaicism), allowing immediate phenotypic evaluation in the $F_{0}$ generation without waiting several generations to establish a stable line.

### Microinjection in *C. elegans*:

- **Purpose:** Used to introduce **DNA, RNA, proteins, or CRISPR/Cas9** components into germline or somatic cells for **transgenesis**, **RNAi** (by injecting dsRNA into the gonad), and **genome editing**, enabling **heritable expression** of transgenes or edits.

- **Mechanism:** Transgenic or edited animals are generated by injecting appropriate DNA fragments, plasmids, or CRISPR/Cas9 complexes directly into the **distal gonad syncytium**. Because the gonad syncytium shares a common cytoplasm, the injected material is taken up by developing oocytes, which are subsequently fertilized in the **spermatheca** to give rise to transgenic or gene-edited embryos.

**Key point:** the **distal gonad is a syncytium** (shared cytoplasm), so a single injection reaches many future oocytes and gives many transgenic/edited progeny.

### Site-specific screening of insertional mutant libraries

**Genotype analysis for site-specific screening of insertional mutant libraries:** When looking for mutations in a specific gene ("Your Favorite Gene", **YFG**) within an insertional mutant library (e.g., carrying **Tc3** transposon insertions):

- Design **PCR primers** flanking the genomic region of interest and amplifying the target region from the genome of insertional mutants.

- Compare the size of the PCR products: a **normal (wild type) gene** gives the expected amplicon size, whereas a **mutated gene with an insertion** yields a **larger PCR product** due to the inserted *Tc3* transposon sequence.

- **Important Note:** This allows for **rapid screening of mutant libraries** to identify strains carrying insertions in the gene of interest.

**In short:** a **random method** (an insertional library) used in a **reverse-genetics** way: instead of asking "which gene causes this phenotype?", you ask "**which strain has an insertion in my gene?**".

### Post-Transcriptional Gene Silencing: RNA Interference (RNAi)

**Overview and molecular mechanism of RNAi (Dicer and RISC).** The mechanism of RNA interference was first discovered in *C. elegans* and is a cornerstone tool for reverse genetics and high-throughput functional screens (Conte et al., *Curr. Protoc. Mol. Biol.* 2015). RNAi downregulates specific genes by introducing **double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)** molecules matching the sequence of the target gene:

- **Step A (dsRNA recognition):** Long **double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)** matching the target gene sequence enters the cell and is recognized by the RNase III endoribonuclease protein **Dicer**.

- **Step B (Dicer cleavage):** **Dicer** binds and cleaves the long dsRNA into short (~21–23 nt) double-stranded fragments.

- **Step C (siRNA and RISC assembly):** The resulting **small interfering RNA (siRNA)** duplexes associate with proteins to form the **RNA-Induced Silencing Complex (RISC)**.

- ![[FG-image17.png|292]]**Step D (Passenger strand cleavage / RISC activation):** Inside **RISC**, the siRNA duplex is unwound and the sense (**passenger**) strand is cleaved and discarded, leaving the single-stranded antisense guide RNA bound to the active RISC complex.

- **Step E (Target mRNA recognition):** The single-stranded guide RNA directs the activated **RISC** complex to bind by sequence complementarity to the endogenous **target mRNA** (carrying a $3'\text{~AAAAAAA}$ poly-A tail).

- **Step F (Target mRNA cleavage):** **RISC** endonucleolytically cleaves the **target mRNA**, causing rapid transcript degradation, protein depletion, and a post-transcriptional loss-of-function phenotype.

**In short:** **dsRNA → Dicer → siRNA → RISC → cleavage of the matching mRNA**. The gene is intact; its message is destroyed (**post-transcriptional** loss of function).

**Three experimental methods to deliver RNAi in *C. elegans* (using the L4440 plasmid):** In all three methods, the target gene segment (**YFG**) is cloned between **two convergent T7 RNA polymerase promoters** inside the **L4440 plasmid** (which carries an $\text{Amp}^{\text{R}}$ selection marker):

1.  **Microinjection of dsRNA:** Synthesize dsRNA from the L4440 plasmid by *in vitro* transcription with **T7 polymerase**, microinject the dsRNA into the germ cells/gonad of adult hermaphrodites (panels A–H), and analyze the $F_{1}$ progeny for the mutant phenotype. **Advantages:** Highly reproducible from one individual to another, and only a few nanograms of dsRNA are needed. **Disadvantages:** Fairly labor-intensive.

2.  **Soaking in dsRNA solution:** Synthesize dsRNA by *in vitro* transcription with T7 polymerase, soak embryos or worms directly in a tube containing the dsRNA solution, transfer them to plates, and look for phenotypes.

3.  **Feeding *C. elegans* with bacteria expressing dsRNA:** Transform the L4440 plasmid (under Amp selection) into ***E. coli* strain HT115 (**$\text{RNase~III}^{-}$**)**—a mutant bacterial strain that lacks RNase III (preventing the bacteria from degrading the dsRNA) and carries an **IPTG-inducible T7 polymerase** gene. Plate the HT115 bacteria with **IPTG** to induce bidirectional transcription and dsRNA production inside the bacteria, add worms to feed on the bacterial lawn, and score for mutant phenotypes.

**Why HT115 and why feeding:** HT115 is **RNase III⁻**, so it does not degrade the dsRNA it produces. Feeding is the method that makes **genome-wide screens** possible: one bacterial clone (one plate) per gene.

- **Analysis of RNAi effects in individual cells (GFP-reporter strain PD4251):** Strain PD4251 expresses two body-wall muscle transgenes driven by the *myo-3* 5' promoter: a **nuclear-localized GFP–LacZ fusion** (myo-3 5' :: NLS :: gfp :: lacZ) and a **mitochondrially targeted GFP** (myo-3 5' :: MtLS :: gfp):

**Take-home:** **only double-stranded RNA silences** (sense or antisense alone do nothing), and silencing is **sequence-specific**: ds-lacZ removes only the GFP fused to LacZ, not the mitochondrial GFP in the same cells.

- **Nobel Prize (2006):** **Andrew Z. Fire** and **Craig C. Mello** were awarded the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine *"for their discovery of RNA interference – gene silencing by double-stranded RNA"*.

### Gene Reporters in *C. elegans* (GFP and LacZ)

**Important Note.** Transgene expression in *C. elegans* is monitored using **reporters**, which indicate activity through **fluorescence** or **enzymatic signals** (e.g., **GFP, LacZ**).

**Two types of reporter constructs:**

- **A. Transcriptional reporters (promoter** $\rightarrow$ **ATG–gfp–STOP** $\rightarrow$ **3' UTR):** The promoter of the gene of interest drives GFP expression directly to show when and where the gene is transcribed.

> ![[FG-image19.png|481]]

- **B. Translational reporters (promoter** $\rightarrow$ **ATG–\[Exons/Introns fused to gfp\]–STOP** $\rightarrow$ **3' UTR):** The reporter gene is fused **in-frame** with the coding sequence of the gene of interest, producing a chimeric fusion protein that reveals subcellular protein localization.

**In short:** **transcriptional reporter** → when and where the gene is transcribed; **translational reporter** → where the protein is inside the cell.

> ![[FG-image20.png|481]]

**Applications and Nobel recognition:**

- **Live neuronal imaging:** GFP fusions allow visualization of the entire nervous system or specific chemosensory neurons, and combinations of different fluorescent markers can label distinct cell types simultaneously.

- **Historical milestone (Chalfie et al., *Science* 1994):** Marked the **first time GFP was used in a multicellular organism**; because exogenous substrates and cofactors are not required for GFP fluorescence, it can monitor gene expression and protein localization directly in living organisms.

- **Nobel Prize in Chemistry (2008):** Awarded equally (1/3 each) to **Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie, and Roger Y. Tsien** *"for the discovery and development of the green fluorescent protein, GFP"*.

### *C. elegans* as a Model for Parkinson's Disease (transgenic disease model)

#### Parkinson's Disease ($\mathbf{\alpha}$-Synuclein Aggregation and Toxicity)

**Pathway of** $\alpha$**-synuclein aggregation and neurodegeneration:**

- **Aggregation cascade:** Membrane-bound $\alpha$-helical $\alpha$-synuclein dissociates from membranes into natively unfolded monomers, which misfold into toxic $\beta$**-sheet-like oligomers**, **fibrils**, and **Lewy bodies**, leading to the impairment of **dopaminergic neurons**.

- **Modifiers enhancing aggregation:** Neuronal activity $\rightarrow$ PLK2 and other kinases (**phospho-Ser129**); **Calpain** cleavage (**truncation**); inflammation and nitric oxide (**NO** $\rightarrow$ **nitration**); and **ubiquitin** addition.

- **Cellular toxicity mechanisms:** Blocked ER–Golgi transport, ER stress, and Golgi fragmentation; decreased synaptic vesicle release; impaired energy production and apoptosis induction; accumulation of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) substrates and proteasome impairment.

- **Potential therapeutic opportunities:** Knockdown of total $\alpha$-synuclein; limiting the formation or removing oligomers/aggregates (via kinase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, or anti-inflammatory agents); improving organelle function; and limiting cell death.

**Advantages and experimental readouts in *C. elegans*:**

- *C. elegans* allows the study of $\alpha$-synuclein aggregation in the context of a multicellular organism, and aggregation can be specifically analyzed in **dopaminergic neurons**.

- **Fluorescence imaging across aging (Panel A, days 3, 5, 11, 13, 16):** Control worms expressing **YFP** alone show diffuse fluorescence throughout aging, whereas worms expressing $\alpha\text{-syn-YFP}$ progressively accumulate discrete fluorescent aggregates with age.

- **Western blot analysis (Panels B & C, days 3, 5, 11, 17):** Immunoblotting with **LB509** (anti-$\alpha$-synuclein) and **Anti-GFP** antibodies in $\alpha\text{S}$ transgenic worms compared to **N2** wild-type controls and **YFP** controls (using $\alpha$**-actin** as a loading control) detects intact $\alpha\text{S-YFP}$ and reveals age-dependent **degradation/truncation** products at day 17.
