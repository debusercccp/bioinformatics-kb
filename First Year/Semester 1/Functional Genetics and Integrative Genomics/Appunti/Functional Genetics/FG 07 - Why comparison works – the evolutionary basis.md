# Why comparison works: the evolutionary basis

### Common evolutionary history

Living organisms descend from a **common ancestor** and, throughout evolution, have conserved **genetic material, metabolic pathways and developmental processes**. This conservation is what makes it legitimate to extrapolate from one organism to another.

**Darwin and the tree of life:** all living species belong to a single genealogical tree produced by **descent with modification**. New species descend from ancestral species through the accumulation of modifications that represent the adaptation of populations to new environments. At the root of the tree is **LUCA (Last Universal Common Ancestor)**, from which the three domains branch: **Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya**. The differences between present-day genomes have accumulated over about **3 billion years**.

### Developmental biology as evidence of homology

**Ontogeny** is the development of the individual and the transformations it undergoes during its life history. Embryonic developmental biology highlights **homologies that are not visible in adults**: terrestrial vertebrates evolved from aquatic ancestors and retain some of their characteristics early in development (**pharyngeal pouches, tubular heart**), showing that **developmental genes are conserved**. Even very distant species share a common evolutionary history, for example through **homeotic genes** in *Drosophila* and humans.

The **Ernst Haeckel** quote, **"ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny"**. *Further detail:* today this is regarded as a historical simplification: embryos do not literally replay the adult stages of their ancestors, but early embryonic stages are more conserved than adult ones (von Baer).

### Homology and analogy

**Homologies:** similarities between species that share a common ancestor.

- ![[FG-image11.png|366]]**Anatomical homologies:** basic structures, with the **same embryonic origin**, that have been modified over evolution. The example is the **tetrapod limb**, with the common plan **stylopodium** (humerus/femur), **zeugopodium** (radius and ulna/tibia and fibula), **autopodium** (hand/foot), adapted for flight, swimming, running or grasping.

![[FG-image12.png|190]]

- **Analogies:** **not all structural similarities are homologies**. Analogous structures look morphologically and/or functionally similar but have **different embryonic origins** (classic example: an insect wing and a bird wing, the result of **convergent evolution**).

**Molecular homologies:** the "language" of the genetic code is transmitted to all branches of the evolutionary tree. One example is the **ClustalW alignment** of the amino acid sequence of **beta-actin (ACTB)** in skate, zebrafish, human, leopard shark, chicken, *Xenopus tropicalis*, amphioxus and yeast: the protein is recognisable and highly conserved across the whole tree. **Orthologous proteins are conserved among model organisms** and **essential proteins are more highly conserved** across species (substitutions are selected against because they damage a vital function).

As a rule of thumb, if the percentage of homology is lower than 50% the species is a poor choice as a model for the gene under study.

### Homeotic genes and collinearity

**Homeotic (Hox) genes** control segment identity along the antero-posterior axis. The slide stresses **"triple collinearity"**: the order of genes along the chromosome corresponds to

1.  their **chromosomal position**, (they are organized in clusters)

2.  their **temporal expression**,

3.  the **body parts they control**.

It is an exceptional example of conservation of a genomic and functional organization between fly and vertebrates.

### Orthologues and paralogues

- **Orthologous genes:** genes in different species that derive from a **speciation event** from a gene of the common ancestor. They tend to keep the same function and are the basis for transferring knowledge between organisms.

- **Paralogous genes:** genes that derive from **gene duplication** within a genome. After duplication one copy can acquire new or specialised functions (neo/sub-functionalisation), with possible redundancy that can mask phenotypes in knockouts.

*Further detail:* vertebrates have several Hox clusters (paralogues generated by genome duplications), whereas *Drosophila* has only one; for this reason, a comparison must always distinguish orthologues from paralogues.

When a gene has more than one paralogue, all of them should be studied, because redundancy between paralogues can mask the phenotype of a single knock-out.

### Synteny

- **Syntenic genes:** genes located on the same chromosome.

- **Synteny (in the evolutionary sense used in the lecture):** chromosomal fragments **conserved across different species**. Some genome regions remain intact throughout evolution.

![[FG-image13.png|273]]

In the diagram, organism A has genes 1-2-3-4-5-6 and organism B has 7-2-3-4-8-9: the block 2-3-4 is a **syntenic block** because it conserves order and proximity. **The more closely two species are related, the greater the degree of synteny.** Tools such as **Ensembl** let you visualise these conserved regions (the slide shows human chromosome 1 and the corresponding regions in the chicken, *Gallus gallus*, scattered over several chromosomes, together with the vertebrate phylogenetic tree).

Synteny is useful to identify **orthologues reliably** and to know where to look for a homologous gene.

### Cross-species complementation (rescue)

A powerful functional proof: **human orthologues can complement biological functions in model organisms**. Start with a model organism **mutant** for gene A, showing a mutant phenotype. Introduce **the human gene A**: if the wild-type phenotype is restored (**rescue**), the human gene is functionally equivalent to the model organism's one. This shows that the function has been conserved for hundreds of millions of years.

In short, **the greater the number of homologies between two species, the more closely related they are**.
