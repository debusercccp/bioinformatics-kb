# Human molecular variability: general concepts

## 1.1 What is human genetic variation?

**Human genetic variation** is the set of differences in DNA sequence among individuals and populations. At the molecular level (**molecular variability**) it is the presence of different variants at specific genomic positions or across larger genomic regions. The central questions are *how* and *why* different copies of the genome differ among the individuals of present-day populations, and *which biological processes* have shaped this variation.

- About **99.9%** of the genome is the same among modern humans (human vs. human is roughly 99.5–99.9% similar; human vs. chimpanzee roughly 97–98%).
- No two individuals are genetically identical, with the exception of **monozygotic twins**.
- Even this small amount of difference is enough to make each person unique and to generate variation within and among human populations.
- The study of human genetic variation has both **evolutionary significance** and **medical implications**.

## 1.2 Types of variation between human genomes

Variants can be arranged by the size of the genomic segment involved, from a few base pairs up to whole chromosomes.

| **Type** | **Scale** | **Description** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Single nucleotide substitutions (SNVs / SNPs) | Single base | Change of one nucleotide at a specific position. Includes transitions and transversions. |
| Small insertions and deletions (indels) | Few base pairs | Loss or addition of one or a few nucleotides. |
| Micro- and mini-satellites | Few bp to a few kb | Short DNA sequences repeated in tandem a variable number of times (e.g. a single repeat-unit decrease). |
| Mobile elements | Tens of bp to a few kb | Sequences that can change position and/or copy themselves within the genome (e.g. Alu and LINE-1 element insertions). |
| Structural variants (including CNVs) | Kb to whole chromosomes | Major rearrangements: inversions, duplications, deletions, translocations of large segments. At the largest scale: chromosomal translocations, trisomy, monosomy. |

## 1.3 Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)

- SNPs are the **most common** type of genetic variant among people, about **80–90%** of variation. Each one is a difference at a single nucleotide.
- They occur on average about **once every 0.3–1 kb**. With a genome size of 3.5 × 10⁹ bp, the total is around **5–10 million** SNPs.
- They can lie in coding regions (genes) and in non-coding regions.
- Classification of variants within a coding region:
  - **Synonymous (silent):** because the genetic code is redundant, many nucleotide changes do not alter the amino acid sequence.
  - **Non-synonymous:** the amino acid sequence changes. This includes **missense** mutations (a change that replaces one amino acid with another) and **nonsense** mutations (a change that turns a coding codon into a stop codon).
- Most SNPs fall **outside genes** and usually have no effect on cell function or on the individuals carrying them: they are **neutral**.

## 1.4 Structure of the human genome

| **Component** | **Key facts** |
|:---|:---|
| Nuclear genome | 3.2 Gb (3.2 billion bp), located in the cell nucleus, 2 copies per cell. Autosomes (pairs 1–22) plus the sex chromosomes X and Y. |
| Genes and gene-related sequences | About 25% of the nuclear genome. Of this, coding DNA is only about 1.5% and non-coding DNA (pseudogenes, gene fragments, introns, UTRs) about 23.5%. |
| Extragenic DNA | About 75%. Of this, about 21% is unique or low-copy-number and about 54% is moderately to highly repetitive. |
| Repetitive DNA | Interspersed repeats (about 45%: LINE, SINE, LTR, DNA transposons) and tandem repeats (about 9%: satellite DNA, minisatellites, microsatellites). About 50% of the genome is repetitive. |
| Mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) | 16.6 kb (16,569 bp), circular, 37 genes: 13 polypeptide-encoding genes, 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA genes. Hundreds of copies per cell, located in the cytoplasm. |

Key messages: protein-coding regions account for **less than 2%** of the genome, **most of our DNA is non-coding**, and about **50%** is repetitive sequence.

## 1.5 Inheritance of the genome

| **Region** | **Inheritance** | **Recombination** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Autosomes | From both parents | Recombine in both men and women. Reshuffled every generation. |
| X chromosome | From both parents | Recombines only in women (in men there is a single X). |
| Y chromosome | Paternal: father to son (patrilinear) | Does not recombine, except in the PAR (pseudoautosomal) regions shared with the X. |
| Mitochondrial DNA | Maternal: mother to child (matrilinear) | Escapes recombination. Paternal mitochondria from the sperm do not persist after fertilization. |

- **mtDNA** and the **Y chromosome** are the *non-recombining* parts of the genome. They show **uniparental inheritance** and each traces a single line of descent.
- **Autosomal DNA** gives each individual a different combination of traits inherited from both maternal and paternal lines (random recombination).
- Most genetic variation is **inherited** (rather than arising independently in each individual), and its history is shaped by evolutionary forces.

## 1.6 How diversity is distributed

Human genetic diversity has a peculiar distribution within and between populations. Early studies partitioned variation at autosomal loci as follows.

| **Locus type** | **Within population (%)** | **Between populations within groups (%)** | **Between groups (%)** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| 17 classical polymorphisms | 85.4 | 8.3 | 6.3 |
| 30 microsatellites | 84.5 | 5.5 | 10.0 |
| 79 RFLPs | 84.5 | 3.9 | 11.7 |
| 60 microsatellites | 87.9 | 1.7 | 10.4 |
| 30 SNPs | 85.5 | 1.3 | 13.2 |
| 21 Alu insertions | 82.9 | 8.2 | 8.9 |

- Overall, about **83–88%** of autosomal variation is found among individuals *within* populations and only about **9–13%** *between* populations or continental groups. The summary figure gives roughly **85% within populations, 5% among populations and 10% among population groups**.
- Comparison of F<sub>ST</sub> values: humans (worldwide) have a low F<sub>ST</sub>, comparable to waterbuck and impala (Kenya), and far lower than coyote, elephant, Grant's gazelle and especially the gray wolf (Eurasia), which has the highest value shown.
- Conclusion: **low genetic diversity and little population differentiation** in humans.

## 1.7 Evolutionary history of our species

Both phenotypic and molecular variation are influenced by many aspects of our evolutionary past. *Homo sapiens* is a **relatively young species**: anatomically modern humans evolved approximately **200–300 kya**. How the species originated and dispersed is therefore essential to understand its genetic variability.

**Out-of-Africa model (Out of Africa III hypothesis).** Recent origin of *H. sapiens* in Africa plus recent migrations out of Africa led to the progressive substitution of other human species. Anatomically modern humans originated in Africa (200–300 kya) and from around **60–70 thousand years ago** expanded into the rest of the world through a **serial founder effect**.

Approximate dates on the migration map:

| **Event** | **Time (years ago)** |
|:---|:---|
| Origin of modern humans in Africa | About 200,000 |
| Exit from Africa | About 70,000–50,000 |
| Arrival in Australia / Solomon Islands region | About 50,000 |
| Expansion into Europe and into East Asia | About 45,000–35,000 |
| People of the far north-east of Asia / entry to the Americas | About 20,000–15,000 |
| Arrival in South America | About 15,000–12,000 |
| Arrival in Taiwan / Philippines region (Austronesian expansion) | About 3,500 |
| Arrival in Polynesia | About 2,500 |

**Serial founder model.** A starting population with high diversity gives rise to a small founder population with reduced diversity. That founder population expands, and from it a second small founder population forms, with a further reduction in diversity. Repeated over the course of the expansion, this leaves less diversity in populations that are farther from Africa.

**Other factors shaping current variation.** Current patterns of variability reflect **demographic** processes, **adaptive** processes and **cultural** factors:

- **Demographic:** expansions or contractions, migrations and admixture (for example the Bantu expansion within Africa about 5 kya, and the trans-Atlantic slave trade about 400 years ago).
- **Adaptive:** occupation of a wide range of very different environments, which exposed populations to diverse selective pressures: arctic environments, high altitude (Andes, Tibet, Ethiopian highlands), skin colour at different latitudes, pathogens (malaria and trypanosome resistance in Africa), and body-size traits.
- **Cultural and dietary:** dietary shifts, such as fat-rich diets in the Arctic, **lactase persistence** associated with milk consumption (Europe and East Africa), and starch-rich diets (e.g. rice-based in Asia). The map also links high BMI to Polynesia.
