# Key concepts in population genetics

## 2.1 Definition and aims

**Population genetics** investigates biological inheritance patterns and changes in genetic composition within and among populations, to study the processes that shape and modify genetic variation. The perspective shifts from single organisms to **large groups of individuals**: populations are the fundamental units of analysis, and changes in **genetic composition** are examined over time and space.

Typical questions: how does genetic variation arise? How is it distributed within and among populations? How does it change over time and space? Which processes generated it?

- **Population:** a group of individuals within a species that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature.
- **Gene pool:** the total set of alleles present in the population.
- **Genotype frequency:** the proportion of individuals in a population that have a certain genotype (count the individuals with that genotype and divide by the total number of individuals).
- **Allele frequency:** the proportion of a specific allele in the gene pool of the population.

## 2.3 Real vs. ideal populations

The aim is to study **how allele frequencies change in space and time** and to understand the mechanisms that may have acted. Real populations are too complex, so models require **mathematical approximation**. Mathematical models give a formal description of the **expected behaviour** of genetic variation **under specific assumptions**, and act as simplified reference frameworks (**null models**) against which empirical observations can be compared.

The **ideal population** is:

- panmictic (random mating);
- made of discrete, non-overlapping generations;
- characterized by identical allele frequencies in males and females;
- of very large (infinite) size;
- closed to migration;
- free of mutation effects (mutations can be ignored);
- not affected by natural selection on allele frequencies.
