# GANS: Generative Adversarial Networks

> From the training dataset, the other one is the modelled dataset generated by G.
>
> GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) are a **generative modeling technique** — just like VAEs — but they’re built around an adversarial concept: Two neural networks, a **Generator** and a **Discriminator**, are locked in a game of cat and mouse.

- The **Generator (G)** is like a counterfeiter trying to create **fake currency** (or images, sounds, etc.) that look real.

- The **Discriminator (D)** is like a police officer trying to tell the **difference between real and fake money**.

> The goal of training is to make the Generator so good at creating "fakes" that the Discriminator can’t tell them apart from the real thing.
>
> ![[AML-image398.jpg|531]]

1.  **The Generator (G)** starts with random noise (a vector of numbers) and generates a “fake” image from it.

2.  **The Discriminator (D)** is given both real images from the training set **and** the fake images from G.

3.  D tries to correctly label which ones are **real** and which are **fake**.

4.  G tries to **fool** D by generating better and better fake images.

5.  Both networks **learn simultaneously**:

> o D gets better at spotting fakes. o G gets better at generating realistic data.
>
> This is called a **minimax game**:

- The Generator **minimizes** the probability that D catches it.

- The Discriminator **maximizes** its ability to catch G.

> The analogy — learning through **trial, feedback, refinement, and repetition** — fits GANs perfectly:

1.  **Generator tries** to perform a task (generate data).

2.  **Discriminator evaluates** that attempt and provides **feedback** (is it real or fake?).

3.  **Generator adjusts** its strategy based on the feedback — learning where it's weak.

4.  Repeat.

> This **dynamic interaction** (competition) encourages both networks to get smarter, just like in human learning through challenge and correction.
>
> Because the GAN is composed of two networks with different objectives, it cannot be trained like a regular NN. Each training iteration is divided into **two phases**.
>
> **Phase 1: we train the D**

- A batch of **real** **images** is sampled from the training set and is completed with an **equal** **number** of fake images produced by the G.

- The labels are set to **0** for *fake* images and 1 for *real* images, and the D is trained on this labeled batch for one step, using the **binary cross-entropy loss**.

- **IMPORTANT**: the training is quite standard, as per any **NN** (not specifically a D of a GAN), but - in the overall GAN scheme - note that BP only optimises the weights of the D during this phase

> **Phase 2: we train the G**

- We first use it to produce another batch of ***fake* images**

- then, once again the D is used to tell **whether** the images are **fake or real**

- This time we **don’t use a *real*+*fake*** **images** dataset (as for training the D). namely we not add *real* images in the batch, and we set all the labels to 1 (=“*real*”): in other words, we want the G to produce images that the D will (wrongly) believe to be real!

- **IMPORTANT**: the weights of the D are frozen during this step, so BP only affects the weights of the G

> **NOTE**: G never actually sees any *real* image, yet it gradually learns to produce convincing fake images! All it gets is the gradients flowing back through the D.
>
> GAN training for real: **Gen**
>
> We sample some noise (z) using a normal or uniform distribution z is used as input to a Gen, that is able to create an image **x'=G(z).**

- conceptually, z represents the **latent** **features** of the generated image (e.g. color, shape). We do not know which bytes in z are color or shape, though: we do not control the semantic meaning of z (as in any DL classification we do not control the features the model is learning): we let the training process to learn it.

> GAN training for real: **Dis**
>
> Now, a **Dis** looks at real images (training data) and generated images separately. It distinguishes whether its input is real or generated, spitting out **D(x)** as the probability that the input *x* is real, i.e. **D(x)=1 for real and 0 for fake**.
>
> **Dis training**: We train Dis as a Deep NN for classification, and through this process, Dis learns to identify features that contribute to real images, regardless of the source.
>
> At the same time, remember that we want **Gen** to spit out **x’=G(z)** that **Dis** will classify as real, i.e. **we want D(x’)=D(G(z))=1**. **Gen training**: to achieve the above, we train Gen by back propagating the target value all the way back to Gen.
>
> **We train both networks in alternating steps and lock them into a fierce competition to improve themselves** → when Gen creates images **x’=G(z)** about which Dis cannot tell the difference w.r.t real (**x**) ones, the GAN model eventually converges and we get fake images that look like real ones

![](media/media/image401.jpeg)

> Once both objective functions are defined, **they are learned jointly** by an alternating GD

- we fix the Gen parameters and perform a single iteration of GD on Dis using real and generated images

- Then we switch sides: we fix the Dis and train the Gen for another single iteration

- we continue to train both networks in alternating steps until Gen produces good quality images and fools the Dis **GAN applications** The good:

- Generating a high-resolution image from a low resolution image

- Colorisation

- Generate descriptions based on images

- Powerful image editing (e.g., replacing photo bombers with realistic background)

- Turning a simple sketch into a photorealistic image

- Predicting the next frames in a video

- Augmenting a dataset (to train other models)

- Generating other types of data (such as text, audio, and time series)

- Identifying the weaknesses in other models and strengthening them

> **The difficulties of training a GAN**
>
> GAN training is a game. The **Generator** (G) wants to **fool** the **Discriminator** (D), and the Discriminator wants to **catch** **G** faking it. This is called a **zero-sum game** — one wins, the other loses.
>
> The ideal state of this game is when:

- The Generator becomes so **good** that its fake images are **indistinguishable** from real ones.

- The Discriminator is forced to **guess**, like flipping a coin: it can no longer tell real from fake with any certainty (50/50).

> This state is what game theorists call a **Nash equilibrium**: Neither G nor D would benefit by changing their strategy unless the other one changes too.
>
> **It’s Hard to Reach the Equilibrium: the Key Problem is Mode Collapse** Here’s what happens:

1.  The Generator gets **really good** at producing one kind of image (say, cats).

2.  The Discriminator starts getting **fooled** — it sees good fake cats and starts to believe them.

3.  The Generator keeps **doubling** **down** on cats because it’s working.

4.  Eventually, the Discriminator catches on and learns to **detect** **fake cats**.

5.  The Generator shifts to dogs instead but forgets how to make cats.

6.  This cycle continues. The Generator keeps forgetting and relearning — but never gets good at everything.

> **This is “mode collapse”: the Generator collapses to only a small subset (or “modes”) of the data, losing diversity.**
>
> **Other Training Pitfalls:**

- **Oscillation:** Since G and D are constantly adapting to each other, their learning paths might loop or diverge — G improves, D improves, G collapses, etc.

- **Instability:** Training starts well but can spiral out of control — the loss values explode or collapse, and the networks stop learning.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Things like learning rate, batch size, architecture, optimizer, regularization — they really matter in GANs, more than in many other models.

> **Tools and Techniques to Improve GAN Training**
>
> To reduce mode collapse and stabilize training, researchers have proposed clever ideas:

1.  **Experience Replay**

    - Store **generated** **images** from past steps in a buffer.

    - Instead of training D on only the newest fake images, mix in some older ones.

    - Why it helps: It prevents D from overfitting to the latest Generator behavior and helps it “remember” how previous fakes looked.

2.  **Minibatch Discrimination**

    - Normally, D sees each fake image on its **own**.

    - Here, D also looks at the **diversity** of images across the entire batch.

    - If all images in a batch look too similar, D learns to spot that and flag them as fake.

    - Why it helps: It forces G to produce a variety of images — which fights mode collapse.

> **The State of GAN Research**
>
> Even though GANs are theoretically beautiful, in practice they:

- Are hard to train

- Often unstable

- Can suffer from limited output diversity Over time, research has led to huge improvements:

- **DCGANs** (Deep Convolutional GANs): Use convolutional layers — now a standard starting point for image GANs.
